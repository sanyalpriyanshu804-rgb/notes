1. Fundamentals of Electric Charge
Definition and Origin: Charge is an intrinsic property of matter that allows it to exert and experience **Coulombian (electrical) forces**. It is comparable to properties like refractive index or resistivity.

*   **Charging Mechanism:** A body is **neutral** if it contains equal amounts of positive and negative charges, not zero charge. Charging occurs by disturbing this neutrality through the transfer of electrons.
    *   **Positive Charge:** An electron deficiency (losing electrons).
    *   **Negative Charge:** An electron excess (gaining electrons).

# Work function of a body : It is the amount of work to be done on a object in order to remove an electron from it's surface.

Properties:
    *   **Scalar Nature/ Additivity of charge:** Charges are added algebraically (q_{net} = q_1 + q_2 ...). Direction does not matter during addition (but sign does).

    *   **Quantization:** Charge exists only in discrete integral multiples of the elementary charge (Q = ±ne) rather that in continuous amount, where e \approx 1.6 x 10^{-19} C.

    *   **Transferability & Conservation of charge:** Electrons can be transferred from one body to another, but they cannot be created or destroyed

    *   **Association with Mass:** Charge cannot exist without mass. Therefore, charging a body changes its mass: a **negatively charged** body becomes slightly **heavier** (gains electrons), and a **positively charged** body becomes **lighter** (loses electrons).

    *   **Invariance:** Unlike mass, length, or time, the magnitude of a charge is **invariant**—it does not change even if the charge moves at speeds approaching the speed of light.


### **II. Methods of Charging**
1.  **Friction (Rubbing):** Physical contact and rubbing transfer electrons. This method is effective for both **insulators and conductors**. 
# If objects A and B are rubbed the one with lower work function will get positively charged and other will get equal but opposite, negatively charged.

2.  **Induction:** Charging without physical contact. A charged rod brought near a conductor causes **polarization** (redistribution of internal charges).
# works only for conductors and requires earthing
    *   If a positive body is earthed, electrons flow from Earth to the body; if negative, electrons flow from the body to Earth.

3.  **Conduction (Contact):** Charging by touching. For **identical conductors**, the total charge is shared equally: Q_{final} = (q_1 + q_2) / 2.

*   **The Law:** The force between two **point charges** is F = k{q_1 x q_2}/{r^2}.

### **IV. Electric Field (EF) and Motion**
*   **Definition:** The 3D space around a charge where a test charge experiences force.
*   **Intensity (E):** Defined as the force per unit positive (+1C) test charge: $E = F/q_0$.
    *   **Direction:** Points **away** from positive charges and **towards** negative charges.
*   **Motion in a Uniform Field:** A charge q in field E experiences constant acceleration a = {qE}/{m}.
    *   This motion follows standard **kinematic equations**.
    *   If a charge is projected perpendicular to the field, it follows a **parabolic trajectory** similar to projectile motion under gravity.


### **V. Electric Field Lines**
*   **Purpose:** Imaginary lines used to represent the 3D electric field.
*   **Properties:**
    *   Originate at **positive** and terminate at **negative** charges.
    *   **Never form closed loops**.
    *   **Never intersect**, as that would imply two directions of force at one point.
    *   **Density:** The closer the lines, the stronger the field.
    *   **Inside Conductors:** The field is zero; lines must terminate **perpendicular** to the conductor's surface.

# EF due to charge distribution

### **1. General Concepts and Densities**
For continuous distributions, point-charge formulas are insufficient (Coulomb's law), and the field is calculated by integrating over elements. Three types of charge densities are utilized:
*   **Linear Charge Density ( λ ):** Charge per unit length.
*   **Surface Charge Density ( σ ):** Charge per unit area.
*   **Volume Charge Density ( ρ ):** Charge per unit volume.

### **2. EF due to Linear Distributions (Wires and Rods)**
*   **Finite Rod (on axis):** The electric field at a distance $x$ from the end of a rod of length $L$ is $E = \frac{kQ}{x(x+L)}$.
*   **Infinite Wire:** The field is $E = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}$, where $r$ is the perpendicular distance.
*   **Semi-infinite Wire:** At a point perpendicular to the end of the wire, the field has two components, both equal to $\frac{k\lambda}{r}$ (one perpendicular and one parallel to the wire).
    Q=λ(πR)
*   **Angular Ring/Arc (at center):** The field is E = (2kλ​sin(θ/2))/R​, where θ is the total angle of the arc. The direction is always along the **angle bisector**.
    *   **Semi-circular Ring:** At the center, E = 2kλ/R  or  E=2kQ/π(R**2) .
    *   **Quarter Ring:** Or any other ring we can found it.

According to the sources, the electric field (EF) due to a charged thread or rod is calculated based on its linear charge density ($\lambda$) and the orientation of the point relative to its ends.

### **?. General Formulas for a Finite Thread**
For a thread with linear charge density $\lambda$ at a perpendicular distance r, where the ends of the thread make angles θ1 and θ2 with the vertical (perpendicular) line, the electric field has two components:
*   **Perpendicular Component :** This component acts outward (for positive charges) and is given by:
    E_perpendicular = (kλ/r)(sinθ₁ + sinθ₂)
*   **Parallel Component :** This component acts along the direction of the wire (where the angle is greater) and is given by:
    E_parallel = | (kλ/r)(cosθ₂ − cosθ₁) |

### **2. Effects of Symmetry**
*   **Symmetrical Point:** If a point is placed exactly opposite the center of the thread (where θ₁ = θ₂), the parallel components cancel out because cosθ₂ − cosθ₁ = 0. The net electric field in this case is purely perpendicular.
*   **Unsymmetrical Point:** If the thread is not centered relative to the point, both E_perpendicular and E_parallel will exist, and the net field will be the vector sum of these two.

### **Cases**
*   **1: Infinite Wire:** For an infinitely long wire, both angles  θ₁ & θ₂ approach 90. In this case:
    *   E_parallel = 0.
    *   E_perp = 2kλ/r.   {Same as from Gauss' law}
*   **Semi-Infinite Wire:** For a wire that starts at a point perpendicular to the observer and extends to infinity in one direction,  θ₁ = 0 & θ₂ = 90. In this case:
    *   E_perp = kλ/r .
    *   E_parallel = kλ/r .
    *   The net field acts at a 45 angle to the wire.

### **3. EF due to Circular Distributions (Ring on Axis)**
For a ring with radius $R$ and charge $Q$:
*   **On the Axis:** At a distance $x$ from the center, $E = \frac{kQx}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$.
*   **At the Center:** $E = 0$.
*   **Maximum Field:** The field reaches its maximum magnitude at a distance $x = \pm \frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}$.



### **4. EF due to Surface Distributions (Sheets and Plates)**
*   **Infinite Charged Sheet:** The field is $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$.
*   **Key Property:** This field is **uniform** and independent of the distance $r$ from the sheet.
*   **Multiple Parallel Sheets:** The net field at any point is the vector sum of the fields produced by each individual sheet.
*   **Conducting Plates:** When two or more plates are placed together, the outermost surfaces always carry half the total charge sum: $Q_{outer} = \frac{\sum Q}{2}$. Charges on facing internal surfaces are always equal and opposite.

### **5. EF due to Spherical Distributions**
*   **Conducting or Hollow Sphere:** 
    *   **Inside ($r < R$):** $E = 0$.
    *   **On Surface ($r = R$):** $E = \frac{kQ}{R^2}$.
    *   **Outside ($r > R$):** $E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$ (behaves like a point charge).
*   **Non-conducting (Solid) Sphere:** 
    *   **Inside ($r < R$):** The field is directly proportional to the distance from the center: $E = \frac{kQr}{R^3}$ or $E = \frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0}$.
    *   **Outside ($r \geq R$):** $E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$.

### **6. EF due to Cylindrical Distributions (Non-conducting)**
*   **Inside ($r < R$):** The field is $E = \frac{\rho r}{2\epsilon_0}$.
*   **Outside ($r > R$):** The field is $E = \frac{\rho R^2}{2\epsilon_0 r}$.

### **VII. Electric Flux and Gauss Law**
*   **Flux (Φₑ):** The number of field lines passing perpendicularly through an area: Φ = E·A = EA cos θ.
    *   **Positive Flux:** Outgoing from a surface.
    *   **Negative Flux:** Entering a surface.
# Projection rule

*   **Gauss Law:** Total flux through a closed surface is 1/ε₀ times the **net enclosed charge**: Φₑ = Q₍in₎ / ε₀ .
*   **Mathematical Representation**: The law is expressed as the surface integral of the electric field: **$\Phi = \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{in}}{\epsilon_0}$**.
*   **Flux and Charge Sign**:
    *   If the net enclosed charge is **zero**, the net flux is **zero**.
    *   If the net charge is **positive**, the flux is **positive (outgoing)**.
    *   If the net charge is **negative**, the flux is **negative (incoming)**.


*  **Important points - **
    *   Charges located **outside** the Gaussian surface **do not contribute to the net flux**. This is because any field line from an external charge that enters the surface must also leave it, resulting in a net contribution of zero to the flux.
    *   Flux through any closed surface is dependent on inclosed charge only.
    *   The electric field ( E ) used in the Gauss Law formula at any specific point on the surface is the resultant field from all charges (both internal and external).
    *   Flux is independent of the **shape or size** of the Gaussian surface, it only depends on inclosed charge.
# Gaussian surface: An imaginary closed surface chosen using symmetry so that Gauss’s law can easily give the electric field (can technically be any random shape).

### **4. Major Applications (Electric Field Formulas)**
Gauss' Law is used to derive the electric field ($E$) for various continuous charge distributions:
*   **Infinite Charged Wire**: $E = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}$.
*   **Infinite Thin Sheet**: $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$. This field is **uniform** and independent of the distance from the sheet.
*   **Conducting/Hollow Sphere**:
    *   **Inside ($r < R$):** $E = 0$.
    *   **Outside ($r \geq R$):** $E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$.
*   **Non-conducting (Solid) Sphere**:
    *   **Inside ($r < R$):** $E = \frac{kQr}{R^3}$ or **$E = \frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0}$** (field is directly proportional to distance $r$).
    *   **Outside ($r \geq R$):** $E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$.
*   **Non-conducting Cylinder**:
    *   **Inside ($r < R$):** $E = \frac{\rho r}{2\epsilon_0}$.
    *   **Outside ($r > R$):** $E = \frac{\rho R^2}{2\epsilon_0 r}$.



*   **Applications (Field Strengths):**
    *   **Infinite Wire:** $E = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}$.
    *   **Infinite Sheet:** $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$ (constant/uniform, independent of distance).
    *   **Charged Ring (on axis):** $E = \frac{kqx}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$. The field is zero at the center and reaches a **maximum** at $x = \pm R/\sqrt{2}$.
    *   **Spherical Shell or conducting Sphere:** $E = 0$ inside; $E = \frac{kq}{r^2}$ outside.
    *   **Solid Non-conducting Sphere:** Inside, $E$ is **directly proportional** to distance ($E = \frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0}$); outside, it follows the inverse square law.
